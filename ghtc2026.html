Evaluating Multimodal LLMs across Text and Audio Modalities for Accessible Disaster Assistance

Do open-weight multimodal LLMs give the same emergency guidance when a person types a question and when they speak it? We test three models on real alert scenarios for four groups with access and functional needs.

Explore the work Get in touch

Research Background

Effective disaster risk communication is a foundational humanitarian challenge, yet current emergency infrastructure does not meet the needs of many people with access and functional needs, including hard of hearing people, pregnant women, mothers with toddlers, and older adults with dementia. FEMA reports that people with access and functional needs make up to 43% of the U.S. population.

Multimodal LLMs (MM-LLMs) can serve users across text, audio, image, and video in one chatbot-style system. Whether they give consistent, actionable answers regardless of how a user communicates has received little scrutiny. We compare text and audio versions of the same emergency query. The evaluation is small, so findings are indicative, not definitive: no model achieves reliable consistency across modalities, and persona-level consistency is not stable across models. The results inform design recommendations for equitable, trustworthy disaster risk communication tools.

The Study

From real emergency alerts to a text-versus-audio comparison of what each model says.

Framework

Consistency Evaluation Framework

Four components take the study from vulnerable groups to a measured comparison across modalities.

  • Stakeholder identification: four groups chosen from the CDC's access and functional needs list, based on prior literature and high vulnerability in disasters: pregnant women, mothers with toddlers, hard of hearing people, and elderly individuals with dementia
  • Persona creation: 40 prompts built from real IPAWS alert types, such as "I am a [persona], and I just received a flash flood warning. What should I do during this time to stay safe?"
  • MM-LLM inference: each prompt given to each model as text and as audio; one team member recorded the audio prompts
  • Evaluation: semantic similarity between the text and audio responses, plus a factual overlap score

Scope: this study measures informational consistency, whether the model gives equivalent content in both modalities. Audio is passed to the model as a clean digital signal, so it does not simulate the perceptual barriers hard of hearing users face.

Models

Three Open-Weight MM-LLMs

Each model receives the same 40 scenarios for the four personas, as text and as audio.

  • Audio Flamingo 3: based on a 7B language model and the LLaVA architecture, with a unified AF-Whisper audio encoder; handles sound, music, and speech
  • Qwen3-Omni: natively end-to-end and multilingual; processes text, images, audio, and video, and responds in text and natural speech
  • SALMONN: Speech, Audio, Language, and Music Open Neural Network; connects pretrained audio encoders to an LLM through alignment layers
Findings

Where Text and Audio Responses Diverge

Qwen3-Omni is the most consistent across modalities on every semantic metric, and AudioFlamingo the least. Even so, no model is fully consistent.

  • Meaning-based scores (S-USE, S-BERT) are well above wording-based scores (S-BLEU, S-ROUGE): models often keep the core meaning but vary the wording and structure across modalities
  • Factual overlap treats the text response as the source and the audio response as the candidate, using named entities and disaster action terms combined with ROUGE-L
  • By persona, hard of hearing is lowest for Qwen3-Omni and AudioFlamingo, while pregnant woman is highest for both and lowest for SALMONN, so persona-level consistency is not stable across models

Limits and Next Steps

What This Study Does Not Yet Show

The paper treats these results as a first step and names its limits directly.

  • Limits: one speaker recorded all audio prompts; the evaluation is small; personas assume users state their needs, and there is no neutral baseline condition
  • Next steps: diverse speakers and a larger prompt set; finer metrics for safety-critical omissions with significance tests; community surveys and interviews to validate personas; fine-tuning with cross-modal consistency objectives; low-resource languages and multicultural communities
